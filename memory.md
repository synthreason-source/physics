# **The Comparative Dependence of Memory Quality: An Argument for the Relational Nature of Epistemic Evaluation**

## Abstract

This paper examines the proposition:

> **"If it is impossible to compare the quality with other memories, there are either no other memories or no other qualities."**

The statement proposes that the notion of *memory quality* is fundamentally relational rather than intrinsic. Unlike theories that treat memories as independently possessing degrees of fidelity or vividness, this paper argues that quality exists only through comparison within a system of memories. When comparison becomes impossible, either memory itself collapses into singularity or the concept of quality loses all meaning. The argument has consequences for epistemology, phenomenology, cognitive science, and theories of artificial intelligence by suggesting that quality is an emergent property of relational memory structures rather than isolated mental states.

---

# 1. Introduction

Memory is often described using qualitative language:

* accurate
* vivid
* distorted
* weak
* detailed
* trustworthy

These descriptors appear obvious until one asks a deeper question:

**Compared to what?**

Suppose a conscious being possessed only one memory.

Could that memory be considered accurate?

Could it be vivid?

Could it be poor?

Could it even possess quality?

The thesis of this paper is that these questions expose an overlooked assumption in most theories of memory: quality is fundamentally comparative.

Without comparison there exists no criterion under which quality may be defined.

---

# 2. The Central Proposition

We begin with the proposition:

> If it is impossible to compare the quality with other memories, there are either no other memories or no other qualities.

This separates into two exhaustive possibilities.

### Case A

There exists only one memory.

Comparison is impossible because no second memory exists.

### Case B

Multiple memories exist.

However, they possess no distinguishable qualitative dimensions.

Consequently comparison again becomes impossible.

Either way:

Quality disappears.

---

# 3. Quality as a Relational Property

Many physical properties are relational.

Velocity exists relative to reference frames.

Distance exists between objects.

Similarity exists between entities.

Likewise, memory quality may be defined only across memories.

Instead of

> Quality(M)

we have

> Quality(M₁,M₂,...,Mₙ)

Quality therefore becomes a function over relationships rather than individual memories.

This removes the assumption that memories intrinsically contain quality.

Instead, quality is generated through contrast.

---

# 4. The Single-Memory Universe

Imagine a universe containing one observer and exactly one memory.

The observer remembers one event.

Nothing else exists.

Questions become surprisingly difficult.

Is the memory clear?

Compared to what?

Is it distorted?

Relative to which memory?

Is it incomplete?

Incomplete relative to what alternative representation?

Every evaluative statement collapses.

The memory simply exists.

No ordering can emerge.

---

# 5. Quality Requires Difference

Suppose two memories exist.

Memory A.

Memory B.

Now comparison becomes possible.

One may differ by

* detail
* consistency
* emotional intensity
* temporal precision
* perceptual richness

Quality emerges from measurable differences.

Without differences,

A = B

then quality rankings vanish.

Thus comparison requires distinguishable structure.

---

# 6. The Collapse of Quality

Assume comparison is impossible.

There are only two explanations.

### Explanation 1

No alternative memories exist.

Comparison lacks operands.

### Explanation 2

Alternative memories exist but are perfectly indistinguishable.

All evaluative dimensions reduce to equality.

Quality becomes undefined.

Thus:

Impossible comparison

⇔

Memory singularity

or

Quality singularity.

---

# 7. Information-Theoretic Interpretation

Information theory defines information through distinguishability.

If every possible state is identical,

information equals zero.

Likewise,

quality requires distinguishable informational states.

Let

Q(M)

represent memory quality.

Then

Q depends upon Δ(M)

where Δ measures distinguishability.

If

Δ = 0

then

Q becomes undefined.

This mirrors Shannon's observation that information depends upon uncertainty reduction.

Quality similarly depends upon discriminability.

---

# 8. Phenomenological Consequences

Human experience appears continuous.

Yet introspection reveals constant comparison.

We recognize

"I remember this better than yesterday."

"I remember childhood less clearly."

"I remember this face more vividly."

Every judgment references another memory.

Phenomenology therefore supports relational quality.

---

# 9. Artificial Intelligence

Artificial neural networks also evaluate representations comparatively.

Similarity metrics include

* cosine similarity
* Euclidean distance
* KL divergence
* attention weights

Embedding quality exists because vectors occupy relative positions.

An isolated embedding possesses no measurable semantic quality.

Its usefulness emerges only relative to other embeddings.

Thus modern AI unintentionally supports comparative memory theory.

# 10. Formal Statement

Let

M

be the set of memories.

Define a comparison operator

C : M × M → R

If

|M| = 1

then

C is undefined.

If

∀Mi,Mj

Mi ≡ Mj

then

C(Mi,Mj)=0

for every pair.

Quality therefore requires

∃Mi,Mj

such that

C(Mi,Mj) ≠ 0.

Hence

Quality exists

iff

comparison exists.

---

# 11. Epistemological Implications

Truth judgments depend upon comparison.

Reliability depends upon comparison.

Confidence depends upon comparison.

Consistency depends upon comparison.

Without comparison,

knowledge loses its evaluative structure.

This suggests that epistemology itself is relational.

Knowledge is not merely stored.

It is organized through comparative networks.

---

# 12. Relation to False Memories

False memories are usually identified by comparison.

A remembered event conflicts with

* perception
* testimony
* documentation
* other memories

Without these comparisons,

false memories cannot be recognized as false.

Consequently,

truth and falsity become comparative properties rather than intrinsic attributes of isolated memories.

---

# 13. Objections

### Objection 1

A memory could possess intrinsic clarity.

Response:

Clarity requires a measurable scale.

Every scale presupposes alternative values.

Without alternatives,

measurement becomes meaningless.

---

### Objection 2

The brain stores confidence internally.

Response:

Confidence itself emerges from integrating competing representations.

Even Bayesian confidence compares competing hypotheses.

Thus confidence remains relational.

---

# 14. Broader Metaphysical Consequences

If quality is relational,

then many mental properties may also be relational.

Beauty requires alternatives.

Difficulty requires alternatives.

Importance requires alternatives.

Meaning requires alternatives.

Memory quality therefore belongs to a broader class of comparative phenomena.

This aligns with structuralist philosophies in which relations are ontologically prior to isolated entities.

---

# 15. Conclusion

The proposition

> **"If it is impossible to compare the quality with other memories, there are either no other memories or no other qualities."**

reveals an overlooked principle concerning the nature of evaluation. Quality is not an intrinsic feature carried by an individual memory but an emergent relation among memories. When comparison becomes impossible, two exhaustive possibilities remain: either there are no additional memories with which comparison could occur, or no qualitative distinctions exist that permit comparison. In either case, the concept of quality dissolves. Memory becomes mere existence without evaluative structure.

This perspective reframes memory as a relational network rather than a collection of isolated records. It suggests that epistemic concepts such as confidence, accuracy, fidelity, and even truth derive their significance from comparative organization. More broadly, it points toward a structural account of cognition in which evaluative properties emerge not from individual mental states but from the patterns of difference that connect them.
